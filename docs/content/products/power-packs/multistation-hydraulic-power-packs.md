# 3. What is a Multistation Hydraulic Power Pack?

A **Multistation Hydraulic Power Pack** (or Centralized Hydraulic Power Unit) is designed to supply pressurized fluid to multiple, independently operated [Hydraulic Cylinders](/products/hydraulic-cylinders/) or motors from a single central reservoir and pump assembly. 

Instead of a simple up/down single-valve circuit, a multistation pack utilizes a large, CNC-machined [Hydraulic Manifold Block](/products/manifold-blocks/) stacked with a "bank" of directional control valves (typically CETOP 3, CETOP 5, or CETOP 7). 

This centralized architecture is the backbone of automated industrial manufacturing. Whether you need five cylinders to actuate simultaneously with perfectly balanced flow, or sequentially in a strict PLC-timed order (e.g., Clamp → Drill → Eject), a multistation unit handles the entire machine's logic from one location.

# 4. Mandatory Engineering Sections

### 4.1 Typical System Configuration (Multistation Flow)
To understand how one pump drives multiple independent actions without stalling, follow this typical flow configuration:

**Electric Motor** → **High-Flow Hydraulic Pump** → **Reservoir** → **Pressure Filter** → **Main Manifold Block (The Distribution Hub)** → **Directional Valve Bank (CETOP Stack)** → 
  ↳ **Station 1 (Clamping Cylinder)**
  ↳ **Station 2 (Forming Cylinder)**
  ↳ **Station 3 (Ejection Cylinder)**
→ **Return Filter** → **Reservoir**.

*Engineering Note:* By utilizing a single primary manifold with parallel internal galleries, the pump supplies pressurized oil equally to all valve stations. The individual directional valves then decide which cylinder receives fluid based on PLC electrical signals.

### 4.2 Selection Checklist
To properly size a centralized multistation unit, verify these parameters:
- **Number of Hydraulic Stations:** Total count of independent cylinders/motors.
- **Simultaneous vs. Sequential:** Do all cylinders move at once, or one after another?
- **Required Flow Per Station (LPM):** Determines individual valve sizing.
- **Peak Simultaneous Demand:** Sum of flow for all cylinders operating at the exact same time (dictates total pump size).
- **Maximum System Pressure:** Dictates pump type and motor horsepower.
- **Flow Sharing Requirements:** Are proportional valves needed to balance unequal loads?
- **Valve Type:** On/Off Solenoid vs Proportional Directional.
- **Manifold Configuration:** Series vs Parallel circuitry.
- **Electrical Control Method:** Hardwired relays vs PLC fieldbus (IO-Link, Profinet).
- **Future Expansion:** Designing spare blanking plates for future stations.

### 4.3 Typical Design Inputs (Customer Prerequisites)
Before our engineering department can begin drafting your multistation CAD model, we require the following data:
1. **Number of Actuators** (How many total cylinders/motors exist on the machine?)
2. **Individual Flow & Pressure Requirements** (Bore/Stroke for each actuator)
3. **Sequence Logic Diagram** (What is the exact timing and order of operations?)
4. **Peak Simultaneous Demand** (What is the absolute maximum number of cylinders moving at exactly the same millisecond?)
5. **PLC Requirements** (What communication protocol does your factory use?)
6. **Available Installation Space** (For the centralized tank)
7. **Expansion Plans** (Do you plan to add stations next year?)

### 4.4 Engineering Tip
> **Engineering Tip:** 
> Designing spare manifold ports (capped with blanking plates) and specifying extra electrical I/O capacity in your control panel during the initial build is highly recommended. It allows future hydraulic stations to be added to the machine for pennies on the dollar, completely avoiding a massive future system redesign.

### 4.5 Maintenance Snapshot
Centralized systems require diligent maintenance, as a failure here halts the entire production line:
*   **Daily:** Check overall oil level, inspect the manifold block for inter-station leakage, verify all pressure gauges, and observe valve shifting operation for sluggishness.
*   **Weekly:** Inspect all hose routings running from the central pack to the remote stations, verify valve mounting bolt tightness, and check electrical solenoid terminals.
*   **Monthly:** Check pressure/return filter restriction indicators, verify pump-to-motor alignment, and check manifold tie-rod/bolt torque.
*   **Quarterly:** Calibrate all system pressure transducers, perform PLC diagnostic I/O testing, test sequence timing, and conduct an oil particle count analysis.

# 6. Manufacturing: Manifold & Automation Expertise

Building a multistation pack is an exercise in complex precision machining and electrical integration. 

### Multi-Port CNC Manifold Machining
The heart of a multistation unit is the custom [Manifold Block](/products/manifold-blocks/). We CNC machine solid blocks of aluminum or ductile iron, drilling complex internal galleries that route pressure and return lines to multiple valve stations without external piping. This guarantees zero leakage between circuits and ultra-fast valve response times.

### Valve Bank Assembly & Flow Balancing
We assemble stacked CETOP valve banks, often integrating sandwich valves (like flow restrictors or pilot-operated checks) directly beneath the directional valves. This allows us to independently tune the speed and holding pressure of *Station 1* without affecting *Station 3*.

### Electrical Panel Wiring & PLC Integration
A multistation pack is useless without a brain. We build complete, integrated electrical panels pre-wired to the manifold’s solenoids. We program Siemens or Allen-Bradley PLCs to execute your exact sequential logic, ensuring cylinders fire in perfect harmony.

### Simultaneous Load Testing (FAT)
During Factory Acceptance Testing (FAT), we do not just test the pump. We individually test every single valve station. We then simulate your Peak Simultaneous Demand—firing all required valves at once—to guarantee the pump size and manifold galleries can handle the massive sudden flow without pressure drops or stalling.

# 7. Engineering Guide: Designing a Multistation System

Correct multistation design prevents "actuator starvation," where one cylinder steals oil from another.

### Total Flow & Peak Simultaneous Demand
You do not size the pump by adding the flow of every cylinder together. You size the pump based on the **Peak Simultaneous Demand**—the maximum volume of oil required at the exact moment the highest number of cylinders are moving at once. If cylinders actuate sequentially (one after another), the pump only needs to be large enough to supply the single largest cylinder.

### Pressure Balancing (The Path of Least Resistance)
Fluid dynamically takes the path of least resistance. If you actuate two cylinders simultaneously from the same parallel manifold, the cylinder with the lighter physical load will move first, stealing all the oil. To force them to move together, we must engineer **Flow Dividers** or integrate **Proportional Flow Control Valves** to artificially balance the pressure.

### Manifold and Reservoir Sizing
The internal galleries of the manifold must be mathematically sized to handle the Peak Simultaneous flow. If the drilled galleries are too small, fluid velocity exceeds 6 m/s, causing massive heat generation, turbulence, and pressure drop before the oil even reaches the valves. Similarly, the reservoir must hold enough oil to fill all cylinders simultaneously without the pump sucking air (cavitation).

# 9. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Why use one multistation power pack instead of multiple small power packs?**
Centralization drastically reduces maintenance, electrical wiring, and noise. Maintaining one large pump, one set of filters, and one electrical connection is far cheaper and more reliable over 10 years than maintaining five separate standard power packs scattered around a machine.

**How many stations can a multistation power pack support?**
Theoretically, infinite. Practically, we regularly build systems supporting anywhere from 2 to 20+ independent stations. The limiting factor is usually the physical size of the manifold block and the peak simultaneous flow demand on the pump.

**If two cylinders actuate at once, why does one move faster than the other?**
Fluid takes the path of least resistance. The cylinder pushing the lighter load requires less pressure, so the oil rushes there first. To fix this, our engineers integrate flow dividers or pressure-compensated flow control valves on each station to force simultaneous movement regardless of load.

**Can I add another cylinder to my machine later?**
Yes, if planned correctly. We always recommend OEMs design multistation packs with 1 or 2 "spare" stations capped with blanking plates. When you need to expand, simply remove the plate, bolt on a new directional valve, and run hoses to the new cylinder.
